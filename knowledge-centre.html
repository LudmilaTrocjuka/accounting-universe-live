Tronc Monthly Routine: November Tips Are Due by 31 December

A practical checklist for reconciling qualifying tips, approving allocations and preparing tronc payroll before the payment deadline.

Read the published material

Tronc Monthly Routine | November tips are due by 31 December

If your restaurant, pub or hotel runs a tronc, here’s a December checklist to help get qualifying tips and service charges to staff on time.

• By 5 December — Reconcile till reports by site. Send the tip totals and hours worked to the troncmaster and transfer the tips due.
• By 15 December — The troncmaster calculates and signs off each worker’s share.
• By 20 December — Prepare tronc payroll and check the applicable tax codes, National Insurance and pension treatment.
• On or before payday — Submit the tronc FPS to HMRC. On payday, pay staff and reconcile the approved allocations, payroll records and bank payments.

The 5th, 15th and 20th are suggested internal checkpoints. 31 December is the legal deadline for paying November’s qualifying tips. If you pay PAYE electronically each month, HMRC’s deadline is the 22nd of the next tax month.

MTD for Income Tax: qualifying income, not profit

An illustrative sole-trader example showing how gross qualifying income differs from profit and when MTD Income Tax applies.

Read the published material

MTD for Income Tax: your turnover may matter more than your profit.

Consider an illustrative café owner: £42,000 in annual sales, £30,000 in expenses and £12,000 profit.

HMRC uses qualifying income — broadly, gross income from self-employment and property before expenses — to decide when Making Tax Digital for Income Tax applies. In this example, the relevant amount is £42,000, not £12,000.

For a sole trader with no exemption, qualifying income over £30,000 in 2025/26 means MTD starts on 6 April 2027. The next phase, from 6 April 2028, applies where 2026/27 qualifying income exceeds £20,000.

PAYE wages, dividends, pensions and partnership profit shares are excluded from this qualifying-income calculation. LTD company turnover is not included either.

Before making plans, check your Self Assessment figures, possible exemptions and compatible software. Getting the threshold right is the first step.

Late payment: statutory interest and compensation

An illustrative calculation of interest and compensation on qualifying overdue commercial invoices.

Read the published material

LATE PAYMENT DOESN'T HAVE TO MEAN LOST MONEY

Did you know that UK businesses may be entitled to claim statutory interest and fixed compensation when commercial invoices are paid late?

Under the Late Payment of Commercial Debts (Interest) Act 1998, qualifying B2B debts can attract statutory interest at 8% above the applicable Bank of England reference rate.

Let's look at a practical example.

A £5,000 invoice became overdue in October 2026 and remained unpaid for 45 days.

Assuming statutory interest applies:

Statutory interest rate: 11.75% per annum

8% + 3.75% (Bank of England reference rate at 30 June 2026)

Interest calculation:
£5,000 × 11.75% × 45 ÷ 365 = £72.43

Fixed compensation: £70

Total additional amount potentially recoverable: £142.43

The statutory fixed compensation depends on the amount owed:
• Under £1,000 — £40
• £1,000 to £9,999.99 — £70
• £10,000 or more — £100

These amounts are in addition to statutory interest where the legislation applies.

Why does this matter?

Late payments can seriously affect working capital, even in otherwise profitable businesses.

Effective credit control, regular debtor reviews and realistic cash flow forecasting help businesses protect liquidity and reduce unnecessary financial pressure.

Paying staff early for Christmas? Check the date on your payroll submission.

For UK pubs, restaurants and other businesses, December wages may be paid earlier than the normal payday.

HMRC's October 2026 Employer Bulletin confirms a crucial detail: when you pay employees early over Christmas, your Full Payment Submission (FPS) should still show their usual or contractual payday.

For example:
• Actual payment: Friday 18 December 2026
• Normal contractual payday: Thursday 31 December 2026
• Date to enter on the FPS: 31 December 2026

Before December payroll, check:
• The usual payday in employees' contracts
• The payment date entered in your payroll software
• The deadline for submitting the FPS

Could your VAT accounting method be affecting cash flow?

The timing benefits and trade-offs of VAT cash accounting for eligible businesses.

Read the published material

Could your VAT accounting method be putting unnecessary pressure on cash flow?

Under standard VAT accounting, a business may have to pay VAT on a sales invoice before its customer has paid. HMRC's Cash Accounting Scheme can change that timing for eligible businesses.

Consider an illustrative example: a small consultancy invoices £12,000 including £2,000 VAT, with payment due in 60 days. Under cash accounting, the output VAT would generally be accounted for when payment is received rather than when the invoice is issued.

But there is a trade-off: input VAT on supplier purchases is generally reclaimed only after those suppliers have been paid.

The scheme is not automatically advantageous for every business. Restaurants with mostly immediate card takings, businesses with significant unpaid purchases, and those using other VAT schemes may see little benefit or face different rules.

Before changing methods, compare actual payment patterns, supplier terms and VAT liabilities over several periods.

Restaurant break-even: how many covers do you need?

A worked illustration of contribution per cover and the number of covers needed to meet daily fixed costs.

Read the published material

How many covers does your restaurant need to break even on a quiet Tuesday?

A full dining room isn't the only measure of financial health. Knowing your break-even point helps you make better decisions about staffing, opening hours and menu pricing.

Here's a simple illustration (figures are hypothetical):

• Average revenue per cover, excluding VAT: £30
• Variable costs per cover: £15
• Contribution per cover: £15
• Daily fixed costs: £900

Break-even covers = £900 ÷ £15 = 60.

Below 60 covers, the day doesn't cover those fixed costs. Above 60, each additional cover contributes towards profit, assuming the costs and sales mix stay similar.

Your real number depends on food and drink margins, labour patterns, rent, utilities and other costs — and it can change by day of the week.

If hospitality VAT were cut from 20% to 10%, who should benefit — the customer, the business, or both?

Today, a £120 bill consists of £100 revenue and £20 VAT.

If the proposed reduction were introduced:

• Passing it on in full would bring the bill down to £110. The customer saves £10; the restaurant’s revenue remains £100.

• Keeping the bill at £120 would leave £109.09 in revenue and £10.91 in VAT. The restaurant gains £9.09 before costs.

An operator could also share the benefit: reduce menu prices partly and retain the rest to support margins, staffing or investment.

As a restaurant owner or customer, which approach would you favour — and why?
